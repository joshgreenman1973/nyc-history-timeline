# Open questions from the November 2026 fact-check

Claims the checkers could not settle either way. Each line names the card, the field and what was found. Eleven flags, on ten cards, were resolved afterward and are marked RESOLVED; see FACT-CHECK.md for what changed. The rest were left as they were.

- **The Schaghen letter records the 'purchase' of Manhattan (November 5, 1626)**, blurb: Letter date: card says Nov. 5, 1626; Wikipedia's New Amsterdam article dates Schaghen's letter Nov. 7, 1626. Cited NNI page 404s; could not resolve.
- **Wouter van Twiller becomes director-general (1633)**, detail: 'recalled in 1638': Wikipedia and the New Netherland Institute page both say Van Twiller was removed in summer/September 1637 (Kieft named that month); NNI also says he left the directorship in 1638. 'Recalled in 1637' is likely more precise.
- **Jonas Bronck settles the land that became the Bronx (c. 1639)**, detail: 'roughly 500 acres': Wikipedia's Jonas Bronck article gives about 680 acres (274 hectares); cited Bronx County Historical Society page returns 404, could not confirm 500.
- **First ferry links Manhattan and Brooklyn (c. 1642)**, detail: Horn used to summon the ferryman is not mentioned on the cited NNI Breuckelen page (which confirms 1642 and Cornelis Dircksen Hooglandt).
- **Deborah Moody founds Gravesend (December 19, 1645)**, detail: 'The settlement's tolerance later sheltered Quakers': Brooklyn Public Library blog (cited) says the patent's liberty of conscience did not seem to extend to Quakers, though Moody hosted a 1657 Quaker meeting. Possibly overstated.
- **A defensive wall gives Wall Street its name (1653)**, detail: Dutch street name 'de Waal Straat': Wikipedia's Wall Street article gives the Dutch name as 'Het Cingel' ('the Belt'). Could not confirm 'de Waal Straat' from a primary source.
- **The Castello Plan maps a town of 1,500 (1660)**, detail: 'Sent to the Netherlands, it survived in a Medici villa': the surviving Castello Plan is generally described as a c. 1665-1670 copy of Cortelyou's lost 1660 original, not the original itself. Also could not confirm 1,500 people/300 houses (N-YHS page blocked).
- **The Duke's Laws codify English rule (1665)**, detail: Compiler spelled 'Matthias Nicoll' (matches cited HSNYC page), but most sources spell him Matthias Nicolls.
- **The Treaty of Westminster returns New York to England (1674)**, detail: Handover date: card says Nov. 10, 1674; cited America's Best History page says Nov. 11, 1674. Possibly Old/New Style difference; not changed.
- **The Treaty of Westminster returns New York to England (1674)**, detail: Wikipedia's Reconquest of New Netherland article repeats the 'exchange for Suriname' claim, contradicting Wikipedia's Treaty of Westminster (1674) article and the card's cited America's Best History page; fact edit follows the latter two.
- **The Montgomerie Charter reorganizes city government (January 15, 1730)**, detail: Could not confirm James De Lancey 'largely drafted' the Montgomerie Charter; NYPL archives page returned no text.
- **The New York Society Library is founded (1754)**, blurb: 'the city's oldest cultural institution' is not stated on the library's own history page (fetched); could not confirm.
- **The Sons of Liberty organize in New York (1765)**, detail: American History Central attributes the Oct. 31, 1765 boycott agreement to New York merchants, not explicitly to Sears and the Sons of Liberty.
- **The first Liberty Pole rises in the Common (May 21, 1766)**, blurb: Date of the first Liberty Pole is contested. Wikipedia (Battle of Golden Hill) gives May 21, 1766; many accounts give June 4, 1766, the king's birthday, when the pole was raised to mark both the repeal and the birthday (e.g. https://www.geographicguide.com/united-states/nyc/antique/city-hall/liberty-poles.htm discusses both and favors May 21). Consider 'In 1766' or noting the alternative.
- **The prison ships of Wallabout Bay (c. 1779)**, detail: Death estimates vary widely: Wikipedia gives about 11,000 to 12,000; Fraunces Tavern Museum says about 20,000. 'An estimated 11,500' is within range. 'At least 16 hulks' and the nickname 'Hell' for the Jersey were not in the sources I could read.
- **New York becomes the national capital (January 11, 1785)**, detail: Wikipedia's Federal Hall article says the Confederation Congress first met at City Hall on April 13, 1784, which conflicts with other sources (Congress was in Annapolis and Trenton in 1784 and reconvened in New York Jan. 11, 1785). The card's Jan. 11, 1785 date matches unitedstatescapitals.org; the Wikipedia date looks wrong, but the card's cited Library of Congress page returned 403 so I could not check it.
- **The African Free School opens (November 2, 1787)**, date: Wikipedia's African Free School article gives November 2, 1787 in its lead but 'Established in 1794' in its infobox and history section. Nov. 2, 1787 may be the founding resolution rather than the school's opening. Not resolved.
- **Washington inaugurated at Federal Hall (April 30, 1789)**, detail: 'Around one o'clock' could not be confirmed; the National Archives page gives no time.
- **The federal capital moves to Philadelphia (1790)**, detail: Senate source URL https://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/minute/Farewell_NY.htm returns a 404. Replace (e.g. https://www.senate.gov/about/historic-buildings-spaces/meeting-places/philadelphia.htm).
- **Gas lamps light up Broadway (1825)**, blurb: Blurb says the company 'completed' Broadway gas lighting in 1825. Sources say work began May 11, 1825 (todayinsci); CultureNow says Broadway had 120 lamps by 1827, 'making it the first street in New York to be lit completely with gas.' Sources also differ on the stretch (Canal Street to the Battery vs. Grand Street to the Battery). Consider 'began lighting.'
- **Delmonico's opens (December 13, 1827)**, detail: 'Introduced the printed menu' and 'private dining rooms' are common claims for Delmonico's but are not in the Wikipedia article; could not confirm with an authoritative source. Blurb's 'America's pioneering fine-dining restaurant' is also unsourced inflation; consider trimming. Founders were Giovanni and Pietro (anglicized John and Peter).
- **Cholera devastates the city (1832)**, detail: The New York Academy of Medicine source URL (nyamcenterforhistory.org/2015/02/03/cholera-comes-to-new-york-city/) now 301-redirects to an unrelated domain, savethislife.com. Replace or drop this source.
- **The Great Fire destroys Lower Manhattan (December 16, 1835)**, blurb: 'Roughly 674 buildings' pairs a hedge with an exact figure. Wikipedia gives 530 to 700; the city's archaeology site says 'as many as 700.' Consider 'roughly 500 to 700 buildings' or 'as many as 700.'
- **New York Philharmonic gives its first concert (December 7, 1842)**, detail: New York Philharmonic source URL https://www.nyphil.org/explore-more/history/ returns a 404. Replace.
- **Upjohn's Trinity Church is consecrated (May 21, 1846)**, blurb: 'Tallest structure in the city... for decades' and detail 'until the 1890s': Wikipedia's Trinity article says the World Building passed it in 1890, but Wikipedia's St. Patrick's Cathedral article says St. Patrick's 330-foot spires (1888) were 'the tallest structures in New York City.' If so, Trinity lost the title in 1888. Separately, Wikipedia gives the consecration as 'Ascension Day (May 1) 1846,' but Ascension Day 1846 fell on May 21, so the card's May 21 looks right and Wikipedia looks wrong.
- **Cholera strikes again (1849)**, detail: The source labeled 'The Villager' links to thevillagesun.com, which is The Village Sun, a different publication. The label should probably be corrected.
- **The state sets aside land for Central Park (1853)**, blurb: No inline link added. The Central Park Conservancy page confirms state approval in 1853 for land from 59th to 106th Streets but gives no acreage for 1853 ('more than 700 acres'), and it does not mention eminent domain.
- **City fetes the transatlantic telegraph cable (September 1, 1858)**, detail: 'Cutting communication between continents from weeks to minutes': the 1858 cable was very slow. Queen Victoria's 98-word message reportedly took about 16 hours to transmit, so 'hours' may be more accurate for this cable. Not confirmed with a fetched source.
- **The Metropolitan Health Act reforms sanitation (February 26, 1866)**, blurb: 'The first modern municipal health agency in the United States': Wikipedia's Metropolitan Health Bill article calls the law the first New York State bill giving legal grounds for broad sanitary control, and a model for other states. It does not call the board the first such agency. The board was state-created and covered a metropolitan district, not a single municipality, so 'municipal' may be off. Could not confirm or refute 'first.'
- **The first elevated railway opens (July 1, 1868)**, date: Wikipedia (West Side and Yonkers Patent Railway) says 'experimental operations beginning July 1, 1868, and for public use from June 15, 1870'; its infobox gives Feb. 14, 1870 for regular service. 'Began running' on July 1, 1868 holds only as the start of experimental operation.
- **St. Patrick's Cathedral is dedicated on Fifth Avenue (May 25, 1879)**, blurb: 'When it opened it was the largest Catholic church in the United States': not confirmed. Sources call it, in the present tense, the largest Gothic Revival Catholic cathedral in North America. None I fetched confirms its rank in 1879.
- **Chinese Exclusion Act freezes Chinatown's growth (1882)**, detail: 'By 1887 the number admitted had collapsed to about 10': the National Archives page cited does not give this figure. The roughly 40,000 (39,579) for 1882 checks out, but the '10' traces only to a classroom answer key (asianamericanedu.org) and needs a primary source such as the commissioner-general of immigration's annual reports. The NPS page also does not say Chinatown's borders stayed fixed until 1965. It says only that the post-1965 influx expanded the neighborhood beyond its seven blocks.
- **Pulitzer buys the World, launching a press war (1883)**, blurb: No inline link added. pulitzer.org and Britannica refused automated fetches. Search results support the 15,000 starting circulation and a 600,000 peak, but I could not open a page to link.
- RESOLVED: **The Great Blizzard of 1888 (March 11, 1888)**, blurb: Death toll conflict. The card says 'roughly 200' died in New York City, the figure most secondary sources use (Britannica: more than 400 across the region). But the card's own Library of Congress source says 'an estimated 800 people are killed in New York City alone,' and Baruch College's NYCdata page says '400 lives in NYC alone.' Not changed; needs a ruling on which figure to use. No link added.
- **Riis publishes How the Other Half Lives (1890)**, detail: 'Helped drive the... settlement movement of the following decades': New York's settlement movement predates the book (University Settlement was founded in 1886), so Riis can only have helped it grow. Consider cutting 'and settlement movement.'
- **The New York Botanical Garden is founded (April 28, 1891)**, detail: 'The garden opened to the public in 1900': not confirmed. Wikipedia gives no single opening date. It says the first structures opened at the end of the 1890s, the library was finished in 1900 and the conservatory in 1902.
- **Carnegie Hall opens (May 5, 1891)**, detail: 'Renamed Carnegie Hall in 1893': Wikipedia places the renaming in the 'mid-1890s.' Carnegie Hall's own site blocked fetching, so I could not confirm 1893.
- **A cholera scare shuts the harbor (1892)**, detail: Moravia arrival date: an 1892 newspaper report and Marine-Hospital Service records say the Moravia arrived Aug. 31, with 22 deaths on the voyage. The Treasury's cholera circular is dated Aug. 30, and the card says Aug. 30. Not settled from a primary source. Also, the card's New York Academy of Medicine source URL (nyamcenterforhistory.org) now redirects to savethislife.com, an unrelated domain, so that source link should be checked or replaced. No inline link added.
- **American Mutoscope film company is founded (1895)**, blurb: 'First American firm devoted entirely to producing and exhibiting motion pictures' appears only in Wikipedia and its mirrors; no independent source found. Also, the company was incorporated in New Jersey (Dec. 30, 1895), though based in Manhattan, so 'founded in New York City' is loose.
- **Edison's Vitascope debuts at Koster & Bial's (April 23, 1896)**, detail: Could not confirm that the theater began billing the Vitascope as 'Edison's greatest marvel' 'within days': the LOC poster with that title is undated and the phrase appears to have been used in pre-opening promotion. 'American vaudeville debut' of projected film also not independently confirmed.
- **The Greater New York charter is signed (May 4, 1897)**, detail: Could not confirm the charter passed the legislature in 'March 1897' (Mayor Strong vetoed it and the legislature repassed it before Black signed May 4) or that it ran 'roughly 900' pages.
- **The Brooklyn Museum opens its Beaux-Arts building (October 2, 1897)**, blurb: 'Conceived as one of the largest art museums in the world' not confirmed; Wikipedia says only that the original plan was about four times what was built.
- **Tenement House Act reforms housing (April 12, 1901)**, detail: Wikipedia confirms the April 12, 1901 enactment but does not confirm the per-apartment water closet requirement or the creation of a Tenement House Department (both widely reported; the department was created in 1901 by charter amendment, not necessarily this act).
- **New York Stock Exchange opens its Broad St. home (April 22, 1903)**, blurb: $4 million cost is supported by Archiseek and a Real Deal summary of the Times (building near $4 million; about $9 million with land), but not by NPS or Wikipedia. Treat as approximate.
- **Williamsburg Bridge opens over the East River (December 19, 1903)**, fiction.0.note: The Nolans in 'A Tree Grows in Brooklyn' are mostly the American-born children of Irish and Austrian immigrants, so 'poor immigrant family' is loose; 'poor Irish and Austrian American family' would be more precise.
- **General Slocum fire kills more than 1,000 (June 15, 1904)**, detail: Wikipedia calls the Slocum the deadliest manmade disaster in the New York area until 9/11 and notes the official Steamboat Inspection Service count was 957 (1,021 is the common estimate). 'City's deadliest disaster' is slightly broader than the source.
- **Ellis Island immigration hits its peak (1907)**, blurb: Annual total varies: History.com gives 1,004,756 for 1907, while the NPS Ellis Island chronology (https://home.nps.gov/elis/learn/historyculture/ellis-island-chronology.htm) says 1.2 million were examined that year. 'Roughly 1 million' may understate.
- **Singer Building becomes world's tallest (May 1, 1908)**, blurb: Wikipedia gives the Singer Building 41 floors above ground (47 is the figure often quoted, counting the tower's top levels) and says it was razed 1967-1969, while the card says 1968.
- **Metropolitan Life Tower tops the skyline (1909)**, blurb: Wikipedia gives the Met Life Tower 49 floors (early plans called for 50 total), so '50-story' may be off by one.
- **Uprising of the 20,000 shirtwaist strike (November 22, 1909)**, date: The Cooper Union meeting where Clara Lemlich called for a general strike was Nov. 22, 1909; the mass walkout began the next day or two (Wikipedia gives Nov. 24, other accounts Nov. 23). The blurb's 'walked out' fits the later date better.
- **Grand Central Terminal opens (February 2, 1913)**, detail: The 'Tiffany-glass' attribution of the 42nd Street facade clock is disputed: the work is unsigned, Tiffany could not confirm it and no signatures were found during restoration (https://untappedcities.com/inside-the-worlds-largest-tiffany-clock-at-grand-central-terminal). Dropped from the style rewrite.
- **Woolworth Building crowns the skyline (April 24, 1913)**, detail: 'Paid cash' is the popular account, but Wikipedia says Woolworth provided $5 million and investors the rest of the roughly $13.5 million cost. Could not settle it.
- **Equitable Building spurs zoning fight (1915)**, blurb: Story count varies by source: 36 exterior stories, 38 or 40 including penthouse and interior floors (Wikipedia, Structurae). 38 is defensible but not universal.
- **The Great Migration remakes Harlem (c. 1916)**, detail: Harlem's 1920s Black population is given as about 175,000 in the card (NMAAHC source, could not fetch) but 'some 200,000' by History.com (https://www.history.com/articles/great-migration). History.com also calls Harlem formerly all-white rather than planned as an upper-class enclave.
- **The Provincetown Players bring O'Neill to the Village (November 3, 1916)**, detail: The Provincetown group first performed in July 1915 and was formally organized as the Provincetown Players in September 1916; 'formed the previous summer' (1916) is loose. Wikipedia also gives Sept. 19, 1916 as the opening of the 139 MacDougal theater, while the LOC (cited) puts the first New York bill on Nov. 3, 1916.
- **Silent Parade marches down Fifth Avenue (July 28, 1917)**, detail: Wikipedia says the NAACP organized the parade and James Weldon Johnson orchestrated it, with Du Bois marching among the leaders rather than organizing; it also estimates 8,000 to 15,000 marchers.
- **Influenza pandemic strikes New York (1918)**, blurb: 'Roughly 30,000' deaths: the card's own source (University of Michigan Influenza Encyclopedia) gives 20,608 influenza and pneumonia deaths from Sept. 15 to Nov. 16, 1918; other counts of about 30,000 cover a longer period or all-cause deaths. Consider dating or sourcing the figure.
- RESOLVED: **Wall Street bombing kills 38 (September 16, 1920)**, detail: Wikipedia no longer calls it the deadliest terrorist attack on US soil 'to that point' and ranks it below earlier events such as the 1919 Elaine massacre. The claim may need a qualifier ('deadliest bombing').
- RESOLVED: **Air conditioning cools the Rivoli Theatre (May 1925)**, blurb: 'One of the first buildings cooled for public comfort' is doubtful: mechanically cooled movie theaters date to 1917 (Chicago's Central Park Theater; New Empire Theater in Montgomery, Ala.), and Carrier cooled Dallas's Palace Theatre in 1924. The Rivoli was the first New York showcase, per ACHR News and Carrier. ACHR does not confirm the 1902 Brooklyn plant claim in the detail.
- **First Feast of San Gennaro on Mulberry Street (September 1926)**, detail: Claim that cash pinned to the statue was distributed to poorer neighbors is not on the official feast history page; unverified.
- RESOLVED: **The RCA Building opens at Rockefeller Center (May 1933)**, blurb: '70-story': Wikipedia's 30 Rockefeller Plaza article puts the main tower at 66 stories and calls 70 a publicist's figure (the observation deck is on floors 67-70); sources are inconsistent. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30_Rockefeller_Plaza
- **The Village Vanguard opens (February 22, 1935)**, detail: 'early stage hosted beat poetry': Beat poets emerged in the late 1940s-1950s; Beat readings at the Vanguard (e.g., Kerouac) were in 1957-58, not the club's early years. Also Wikipedia is inconsistent on whether the club first opened on Charles Street in 1934 before Seventh Avenue South.
- **The Frick Collection opens on Fifth Avenue (December 16, 1935)**, detail: 'on Museum Mile': the Museum Mile is often defined as Fifth Avenue from 82nd to 105th Street, which excludes 1 East 70th Street; some sources (Wikipedia) include the Frick as its southern end. Also, the Frick describes its holdings as 'Renaissance to the early twentieth century,' not 14th- to 19th-century (https://www.frick.org/about/history).
- **Triborough Bridge opens (July 11, 1936)**, historian.take: Could not confirm that Caro dates the authority's perpetual-existence changes to 1937-38 or uses the quoted phrase 'fourth branch' of government. Check against the book.
- **Triborough Bridge opens (July 11, 1936)**, detail: Wikipedia says Roosevelt attended the dedication; not confirmed he spoke. Also 'Its tolls bankrolled Moses's later parkways, bridges and public works' is broader than Wikipedia, which says toll-backed bonds partly financed the parkway system.
- **Ballet Theatre gives its inaugural performance (January 11, 1940)**, blurb: 'Billed as America's first ballet theatre': not confirmed by the Library of Congress exhibit or Wikipedia.
- **Normandie burns and capsizes at its pier (February 9, 1942)**, sources: Cited Naval History and Heritage Command URL (danfs/l/lafayette-ap-53.html) returned 404 when fetched.
- **Harlem riot follows a soldier's shooting (August 1, 1943)**, blurb: 'leaving six dead': Wikipedia's Harlem riot of 1943 article gives five in its lead/infobox and six in its aftermath section (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem_riot_of_1943). Britannica (cited) not reachable to confirm.
- **Bebop is born on 52nd Street (c. 1944)**, detail: 'The Onyx booking is often cited as the first time the word was used to promote a band': no source found tying the first promotional use of 'bebop' to the Onyx; the sentence also refers to an Onyx booking the card never describes (Gillespie and Oscar Pettiford co-led a group there from late 1943 into 1944).
- **V-J Day erupts in Times Square (August 14, 1945)**, detail: 'one of the largest spontaneous gatherings in the city's history': not checked against a source beyond the 2 million estimate.
- **Idlewild Airport opens in Queens (July 1, 1948)**, blurb: 'dedicated by President Truman that summer': Wikipedia ties Truman's attendance to the July 1, 1948 opening ceremony; other accounts give a July 31 dedication. Wording is safe either way.
- **The Puerto Rican Great Migration peaks (c. 1953)**, detail: 'building El Barrio and Loisaida': the name Loisaida dates from the 1970s, and El Barrio formed in the 1920s-30s, before the early-1950s peak.
- **The Seagram Building is completed on Park Avenue (1958)**, date: December 1, 1958 is unsupported. Wikipedia gives an official opening of May 22, 1958 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagram_Building); Britannica and TCLF say only 'completed in 1958'; SAH Archipedia says it opened in 1959 (certificate of occupancy 1959). Consider '1958' or 'May 22, 1958' after a second source.
- **The Seagram Building is completed on Park Avenue (1958)**, blurb: Setback distance: Wikipedia and SAH Archipedia say 90 feet behind Park Avenue; some other sources say 100 feet. Card says 100. Could not settle with an authoritative source (LPC report PDF was unreadable).
- RESOLVED: **The Cross Bronx Expressway is completed (1963)**, detail: 'evicting an estimated 5,000 families' looks unsupported. Caro reports Moses' estimate of 1,530 apartments housing 5,000 people for the one-mile East Tremont stretch; estimates for the full route run to as many as 60,000 residents (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_Bronx_Expressway; https://urban-renewal-atlas.vercel.app/cities/new-york/cross-bronx). The figure appears to conflate people and families.
- RESOLVED: **The Cross Bronx Expressway is completed (1963)**, historian.take: 'racially integrated East Tremont': Caro portrays East Tremont as a largely Jewish working-class neighborhood; I could not confirm he calls it racially integrated. Worth checking against the 'One Mile' chapter.
- **First New York Film Festival opens (September 10, 1963)**, blurb: Film count: Film Comment says 20 new features; Wikipedia's festival list and MUBI count 21 for 1963 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_films_shown_at_the_New_York_Film_Festival; https://www.filmcomment.com/article/the-first-new-york-film-festival/). Left as 20.
- **Lindsay creates one-stop film permitting (1966)**, blurb: 'first city to actively court location shooting': the city's own Office of Media and Entertainment history says New York was 'the first city in the world to offer one-stop permits,' a narrower claim (https://www.nyc.gov/site/mome/about/office-history.page).
- **Joseph Papp's Public Theater opens with "Hair" (October 17, 1967)**, detail: 'secured the crumbling Astor Library from the city for a nominal rent': accounts conflict. Some say the Shakespeare Festival bought the building in 1965-66 (with Landmarks Preservation Commission help), and the city bought it in 1971 and leased it back for $1 a year; Wikipedia says Papp persuaded the city to buy it. The 1967 'from the city' framing may be anachronistic.
- **The Ocean Hill-Brownsville teachers' strike (1968)**, historian.work: Podair's 'The Strike That Changed New York' was published by Yale University Press in 2002, not 2004 (Cambridge Journal of American Studies review lists 'Yale University Press, 2002'; library catalogs ©2002). Field not in the editable list, so flagged.
- **The Stonewall uprising ignites gay liberation (June 28, 1969)**, detail: 'Figures like Marsha P. Johnson and Sylvia Rivera became central to the resistance': Rivera's presence, at least on the first night, is disputed (David Carter found no credible source placing her there); Johnson by her own account arrived after the riot began. Consider softening.
- RESOLVED: **Arthur Mitchell founds Dance Theatre of Harlem (August 15, 1969)**, date: August 15, 1969 is ambiguous. Columbia Libraries' Arthur Mitchell exhibit says DTH was incorporated Feb. 11, 1969, and that Aug. 15, 1969 is when the Harlem School of the Arts closed its dance department; Encyclopedia.com gives Aug. 15, 1969 as the founding date.
- **Nuyorican Poets Cafe is born (October 31, 1973)**, detail: Purchase of 236 East 3rd Street: Wikipedia says by 1980; the Cafe's own history page says 'By 1981' (https://www.nuyorican.org/history-awards).
- **Soviet Jews make Brighton Beach 'Little Odessa' (c. 1975)**, detail: Source (Brooklyn Jewish Historical Initiative) says nearly 40,000 Soviet Jews arrived 'in Brighton' 1975-80; card omits 'in Brighton', and 'largest Russian-speaking population in the city' is not in the cited source.
- **"A Chorus Line" opens at the Shubert Theatre (July 25, 1975)**, detail: 'Beginning Off-Broadway ... in April' refers to previews (April 16, 1975); Playbill gives the Public Theater opening as May 21, 1975. Also, the official Broadway opening was Oct. 19, 1975, after the July 25 first performance. Defensible as written.
- **Studio 54 opens (April 26, 1977)**, detail: Rubell and Schrager pleaded guilty to tax evasion on Nov. 2, 1979, and were sentenced in January 1980 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Rubell). CBS and others call it a 1980 conviction, so I left the year off the conviction and kept 'prison in 1980.'
- **The AIDS epidemic surfaces in New York (July 3, 1981)**, detail: 'Killed tens of thousands of New Yorkers' likely understates: the city's health department counted 85,292 deaths among reported AIDS cases by 2005 (https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doh/downloads/pdf/dires/dires-2005-report-qtr1.pdf), and NYU Langone cites an expert estimate of more than 100,000 (https://nyulangone.org/news/hiv-positive-new-yorkers-are-living-longer-still-dying-underlying-infection). Consider 'more than 100,000' if a city figure confirms it.
- **The AIDS epidemic surfaces in New York (July 3, 1981)**, reading.0.note: 'With New York at its center' is questionable: Shilts was a San Francisco reporter, and the book ranges across San Francisco, New York, the CDC and Paris. 'Among its main settings' may be fairer.
- **The AIDS epidemic surfaces in New York (July 3, 1981)**, fiction.1.note: Rent is generally set from Christmas 1989 to Christmas 1990 (the film dates it explicitly); 'early-1990s' is loose. Some stage summaries give other years, so not changed.
- RESOLVED: **The AT&T Building is completed (October 1, 1984)**, date: Could not confirm Oct. 1, 1984. Wikipedia says the first occupants moved in July 29, 1983, and the building was 'completed sometime in 1984'; LPC also says only 1984. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/550_Madison_Avenue. Consider '1984'.
- **The AT&T Building is completed (October 1, 1984)**, detail: The 2020s renovation put the public garden in the rear atrium/plaza that Sony enclosed in the 1990s; 'ground-floor arcade converted to a public garden' may conflate the two. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/550_Madison_Avenue
- **The crack epidemic engulfs the city (c. 1985)**, fiction.0.note: Do the Right Thing is about race and policing on one Brooklyn block, not crack; its tie to this card is thin.
- **Tenement Museum founded at 97 Orchard Street (1988)**, detail: Wikipedia gives roughly 15,000 residents from more than 20 countries across both museum buildings; the 7,000 figure for 97 Orchard alone is the museum's own estimate, but tenement.org returned 403 and I could not confirm it.
- **A new immigrant wave remakes the city (1990)**, detail: 'On average, better educated than earlier arrivals' is not supported by the cited New York Fed piece, which says only that some groups (mainly from Asia) have higher college rates than native-born residents while many Latin American and Caribbean immigrants arrive without a high school diploma.
- **"Law & Order" premieres on NBC (September 13, 1990)**, detail: Time has moved on: NBC revived Law & Order in 2022 with season 21, and Wikipedia now lists 26 seasons, so the series has passed Gunsmoke's 20 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_%26_Order). 'At the time' keeps the sentence accurate, but the card might note the revival.
- **Terrorists bomb the World Trade Center (February 26, 1993)**, detail: 'The largest such rescue in city history to that point' is vague and I couldn't find a source for it. Wikipedia says about 50,000 people were evacuated.
- **CompStat and the great crime decline (1994)**, detail: Early CompStat meetings were often described as twice weekly, not weekly; not confirmed, left as is. 'Under 700 by decade's end' not checked against an NYPD table.
- RESOLVED: **"The Lion King" opens on a redeveloped 42nd Street (November 13, 1997)**, historian: Osman's Invention of Brownstone Brooklyn is about brownstone Brooklyn gentrification and has no clear tie to Times Square redevelopment. The take is fairly attributed, but it is on the wrong card.
- **Bloomberg elected mayor after 9/11 (November 6, 2001)**, blurb: Wikipedia's 'Mayoralty of Michael Bloomberg' page calls him the 109th mayor; the city's usual count (Adams 110th) makes him the 108th, as the card says. Left unchanged.
- **Fresh Kills recovery operation ends (July 2002)**, blurb: Tonnage varies by source: the card's own DSNY source says about 1.4 million tons were brought to Fresh Kills and sorted (https://www.dsnyremembers.org/fresh-kills-recovery), but VOA reported 1.6 million tons sifted (https://www.voanews.com/a/a-13-a-2002-07-15-31-new-67433817/384323.html), and other sources give 1.2 or 1.8 million. Final screening was July 26, 2002.
- **Northeast blackout darkens the city (August 14, 2003)**, detail: Con Edison said all power was restored to the city at 9:03 p.m. on Aug. 15, just under 29 hours after the blackout (https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lights-back-on-in-new-york-city/). 'Over the following two days' is loose; 'by the next night' would be more precise.
